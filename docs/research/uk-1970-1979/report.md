# Rocker — UK Music Industry Research Report, 1970–1979

**Purpose.** A first research pass on the UK music industry 1970–1979,
for game mechanics, events and economic balancing in *Rocker*. This
report proposes **10 historically grounded game events** and **5
economic mechanics**; every proposal links to findings (F-numbers) in
`findings.xlsx`, where each row carries a source URL, evidence class and
confidence.

**How to read the proposals.** Each proposal separates:
- *Historical basis* — what the sources support (with finding links);
- *Proposed abstraction* — the game rule, which is our invention and
  labelled as such;
- *Beginning-friendliness* — whether the anchor data is for beginners or
  famous acts (the brief's warning: never treat famous acts as
  representative of beginners).

**The single biggest gap.** Almost every hard money figure for 1970–79
that public sources yielded is about *established* acts (10cc, Gallagher
& Lyle, Dire Straits, Yes, the Pistols). The unknown-band pub fee,
support-slot terms and booking-agent commission remain unsourced (see
`unresolved-questions.md`). The proposals below therefore anchor the
*early* game mostly on self-release costs and wage/dole baselines, which
ARE documented, and reserve the fee figures for the mid/late ladder —
deliberately avoiding the trap of pricing beginners like 10cc.

## Part 1 — Five economic mechanics

### M1. The gig fee is a stack, not a number (per-show cost deduction)
**Historical basis.** In 1974, 10cc commanded ~£1,000 a gig but ~£750
was spent by showtime — road crew, hotels, petrol, vehicle
purchase/depreciation, publicity (F03). Gallagher & Lyle's 29-person
tour cost £15,000 and returned ~£3,000, with hotels £3,500, orchestra
wages £5,000, lorry and coach £1,500 (F04). Of each ticket, ~⅓ went to
the promoter plus VAT (8%) and box office before the band's share (F02);
ticket tiers themselves ran 50p–£3.50 (F01).

**Proposed abstraction.** A gig's payout is computed as: gross fee →
minus promoter/venue share and VAT → minus a *show cost stack* that
scales with crew size, transport and PA (each an upgradeable line item)
→ net to band. Show that stack in the booking quote, so a big-looking
fee can be visibly a loss-maker.

**Why it changes play.** It makes the tour-rig/crew decision a real
economic choice rather than flavour, and preserves the historical truth
that growing acts can be cash-poor (F03 gross-vs-net inversion).

**Beginning-friendliness.** The documented figures are famous-act scale;
the *structure* (costs scale with entourage) is the transferable part,
and early-game entourages should be near-zero (a van and no crew).

### M2. Self-financed releases as the first rung (DIY pressing and recoupment)
**Historical basis.** Desperate Bicycles' 1977 single cost £153 all-in
for 500 copies (3 hours studio, pressing, sleeves) and made £210 profit;
a 1,000 repress sold out in a fortnight (F11). Scritti Politti's sleeve
accounts: recording £98 (14 hrs), mastering £40, pressing £369.36 (2,500
at 13p + £27 plating), labels £8 (F12). Buzzcocks borrowed ~£750 for a
1,000-copy EP (F13). Pressing was priced per unit — 13p/copy (F12) — and
a B-side doubled cutting cost unless both tracks shared one cut (F12). A
studio slot was £27–30/hour or ~£100/day (F14, F28).

**Proposed abstraction.** A `Self-release` action: choose run size
(500–5,000), pay setup + per-copy costs up front, choose studio tier
(provincial day-deal vs London hourly — see M3), then sell through gigs,
shops and mail order with a recoupment curve. Model the documented
failure mode too: the master tape is a separate cash cost (F15: Iron
Maiden lost theirs for £50).

**Why it changes play.** It gives an unsigned band a viable path that is
*cheap but needs distribution* — the honest 1977–78 situation — and
creates the press-your-run-too-small/large decision.

**Beginning-friendliness.** These are genuine beginner-tier figures —
the best-documented early-game anchors in the whole pass.

### M3. The two-tier studio market (cheap day deals vs London hourly rooms)
**Historical basis.** c.1976–77: London 16-track ~£30/hour vs a
Cambridge 16-hour day for ~£100; Studio Sound (Oct 1976) quotes another
16-track room at £27/hour (F14, F28). Sessions were short: Buzzcocks' EP
was cut in 3 hours tracking + 2 hours mixing (F13); Scritti Politti's
record took 14 hours all-in (F12).

**Proposed abstraction.** Studios become bookable locations with a rate
card (hourly or day-deal), a quality factor, and travel cost to reach
them. Cheap provincial rooms make low-budget records rational (F29:
~2,000-record break-even vs ~20,000), while London rooms consume budgets
fast.

**Why it changes play.** The studio choice becomes an economic decision
with real consequences (era-appropriate: the pub-rock/punk
cheap-recording path), and it prices the rehearsal/demo stage too (F28:
demo at ~£27/hr).

**Beginning-friendliness.** Provincial day-deals are exactly how unknown
bands recorded in the late 1970s.

### M4. Deals with options, recoupment and a beginner-tier price (contract model)
**Historical basis.** The Jam signed in Feb 1977 for £6,000 and 6%; an
option exercise over four albums/four years raised the rate to 13%
(F16). Royalty norms: UK 8–15%, new acts at the bottom (F17); base
conventionally ~90% of retail (F18). Recoupment: advances repaid from
the artist's royalty share; recording, video, tour support and some
promo are recoupable; the advance often *is* the recording budget (F21).
Publishing: full-term assignment, 50:50 split, no reversion (F19), with
exploitative standard forms voidable in court — Schroeder v Macaulay
(F20). Management: ~10% era norm, 20–25% of gross as the aggressive end
(F23).

**Proposed abstraction.** Offers are generated with tier-appropriate
advances (new act ~£3–10k with a 5–8% starting royalty; hype acts can
spike far higher — F22), a 1+N option ladder, a recoupment ledger the
player can inspect, and a commission deduction for managers. Onerous
contracts can be challenged in court as rare events (F20).

**Why it changes play.** It replaces a flat "sign and get rich" with the
real tension: a small advance that is also your recording budget, a low
initial royalty that only rises if you hit, and publishing rights you
may regret.

**Beginning-friendliness.** The Jam's £6k/6% is exactly an unproven UK
act at signing; the £40k–£100k punk deals (F22) are hype outliers and
must not be the default.

### M5. Era-gated distribution, prices and certification (market gates that move)
**Historical basis.** Label break-even fell to ~2,000 records for cheap
recordings vs ~20,000 mainstream (F29). Indie distribution
infrastructure (Rough Trade, the Cartel) arrives 1976–78 (F30); the
*official indie chart does not exist until Jan 1980* (F31) — so 1970s
indie releases had to crack the main chart. Charts: BMRB Top 50 from
1969, Top 75 from May 1978, built from ~250 shops (F39). Certifications:
value-based BPI album awards from Apr 1973 (£1m platinum / £250k gold /
£75k silver), thresholds moving over the decade, unit thresholds from
Jan 1979 (300k/100k/60k); singles 250k/500k/1m throughout (F38). Retail
ladder: 50p single / £2.42–2.45 LP (1970–74) → 63p / £2.89 (1975) → 70p
/ £3.49 (1977) → £3.99 LP (1978) → £4.99 LP (1979/80) (F37). VAT steps
10% → 8% → 15% (F40). Singles units did not collapse: 89.1m in 1979
(F32).

**Proposed abstraction.** Distribution reach, chart eligibility, prices
and award thresholds are *date-driven tables*, not constants: an indie
release in 1974 cannot reach national shops the way a 1978 one can;
certification events fire against the era's actual threshold; price and
tax changes step through the decade.

**Why it changes play.** The same record released two years later has
different economics — which is exactly the industry-change feel the game
wants, and it stops any single "correct" strategy from working all
decade.

## Part 2 — Ten historically grounded game events

> Each event = trigger (when it can fire), choice, and consequence. "Basis" links the finding; "abstraction" marks our design.

### E1. "The Friday-night residency" (pub circuit, 1973–76)
**Basis.** London pub venues (Hope and Anchor, Nashville, Tally Ho) drew
crowds from 1973; residencies were the entry rung (F05). Ticket tiers:
50p–£2 (F01); promoter takes ~⅓ + VAT (F02).

**Abstraction.** Offer: a regular weekly slot at a pub venue for N weeks
— low cash, but steady fame growth, a reputation boost with the venue,
and a small chance a scout/agent appears. Missing weeks damages the
relationship.

**Why it matters.** Teaches the beginner that early income is tiny and
relationships are the real asset.

### E2. "Borrow the money" (self-financed first single, 1977–79 window)
**Basis.** £153 for 500 copies (F11); £750 for a 1,000-copy EP borrowed
from friends/family (F13); 13p/copy pressing (F12).

**Abstraction.** When the band has songs and no deal, offer a
self-release financed by a loan (friends/family) with a repayment
obligation; the record can recoup in months (F11) or sit in boxes.
Failure to repay hurts morale/relationships.

**Why it matters.** The historically normal first release; gives a path
that is neither a label deal nor a dead end.

### E3. "The master tape" (session failure point)
**Basis.** Iron Maiden couldn't afford the £50 2-inch master tape and
the studio taped over it (F15).

**Abstraction.** During recording, a cash shortfall event: pay for the
master tape now, or risk the studio wiping/reusing it — losing the
session. Ties directly into M3's studio booking.

**Why it matters.** Small cash traps with permanent consequences make
budgeting feel real.

### E4. "Two nights, one petrol tank" (support slot gamble)
**Basis.** Support-slot *money terms* are undocumented (see unresolved
list) — but supporting bigger acts was a documented route (Sex Pistols
supporting Eddie and the Hot Rods at the Marquee, Feb 1976; F05
context). The safe abstraction: support slots pay expenses or a token
fee, never headline money (flagged as *design inference*, not history).

**Abstraction.** Offer: open for a bigger act — costs you travel/effort,
pays (near) nothing, but reaches their audience (fame gain scaled to
*their* following). Choice: accept, decline, or ask for a fee (risk:
offer withdrawn).

**Why it matters.** It teaches the exposure-vs-income trade-off that
defines early careers; explicitly labelled as inference so it can be
replaced when better data appears.

### E5. "The A&R man in the audience" (discovery, 1971–79)
**Basis.** Discovery ran through the music press (NME 136k→230k weekly;
F26), Peel sessions fed by demo tapes (F24), and live sightings; majors
scouted pub acts hoping for "the next Beatles" (F05 context).

**Abstraction.** Random event after strong gigs/press: a scout offers a
meeting → deal offer generation (ties to M4). Quality gates: recent
strong gigs, demo sent (F28 cost), press mentions. In the punk era
(1976+) the same event can instead offer a self-release deal with an
indie (F30).

**Why it matters.** Discovery should flow from the player's chosen
channels, not pure randomness.

### E6. "Top of the Pops" (chart-gated TV)
**Basis.** TOTP slots tracked songs climbing the Top 40 (F27) — TV was a
*late-stage amplifier*, not an entry channel; regional TV (So It Goes)
was the exception (F27).

**Abstraction.** When a single is charting and climbing, a TV appearance
event fires: big national fame/sales boost, but requires the band to be
available and rested; declining or missing hurts the release.

**Why it matters.** Rewards the moment a release breaks out; models the
era's chart–TV loop.

### E7. "The option comes up" (deal escalation, years 2–4 of a deal)
**Basis.** The Jam: 6% → 13% on option exercise across four albums/four
years (F16); option-ladder deal shapes were standard (F17 context);
label can lock ~25% of publishing (F19 context).

**Abstraction.** At the option date: the label decides (based on sales)
to pick up options, renegotiate upward (better royalty, maybe better
advance) or drop the act. Player can push for a renegotiation;
poorly-performing acts get dropped instead.

**Why it matters.** Makes the contract a living relationship with a
rhythm, and rewards building sales before the option date.

### E8. "The signing spree" (punk-era buzz, 1976–78)
**Basis.** Pistols ~£40k (EMI, Oct 1976; dropped ~3 months later), £75k+
(A&M, 1977; dropped in six days, kept the money); Clash £100k (CBS, Jan
1977) — a 16x spread over The Jam the same year (F22); labels dropped
acts fast (F22).

**Abstraction.** In the punk window, if the band has a buzz stat (press
+ gigs + look), multiple labels bid: offers can be multiples of the
normal beginner advance, but the label's patience is short — poor
first-release performance triggers a drop event (money kept, reputation
hit). Scene credibility can suffer for signing big (design inference).

**Why it matters.** A high-variance opportunity window that rewards
timing, and a risk players will remember.

### E9. "The Cartel starts shipping" (distribution unlock, 1978)
**Basis.** Rough Trade distribution and the regional Cartel form ~1978,
giving indie releases national shop reach; the official indie chart only
arrives Jan 1980 (F30, F31).

**Abstraction.** Late-decade unlock: once the band has an indie release
and a shop relationship, a national distribution option opens — raise
press runs, reach shops beyond gig radius, chart eligibility still
requires the main chart (F31).

**Why it matters.** It is the decade's real structural change for
independents, and it gates the endgame DIY strategy.

### E10. "The band meeting" (human pressures)
**Basis.** Personnel exits driven by money/credits (Rolling Stones,
F35), family and artistic direction (Genesis, F34); the dole and day
jobs underpin the whole early scene (F09, F33); ~300 gigs/year for a
grinding act (F36).

**Abstraction.** Periodic band-meeting event where members raise issues
based on hidden stats (money-split fairness, credit allocation,
exhaustion, family strain from long tours). Player chooses how to
respond (change splits, promise a break, push on); outcomes include
morale shifts, member exit, or a new creative direction. Stated reasons
can differ from hidden ones (F35's contested motives).

**Why it matters.** Keeps the human costs — the brief's area 6 —
mechanically present, not decorative.

## Part 3 — What the data says about the game's current assumptions

- **Advances.** The game's major-label advance range (~£150k–£2.25M)
  matches only superstar tier (F22, punk outliers aside); new UK acts in
  1977 signed for £6k (F16). Recommend a beginner tier of ~£3–10k with
  hype-driven spikes (F22), and reserve seven-figure deals for late-game
  fame.
- **Royalties.** The game's 10–15% major band is in the documented range
  (F17); the 25–50% small-label tier has no UK-major support in this
  pass — keep it for indie/own-label deals only, flagged as design.
- **Costs.** A single recording cost in the low hundreds of pounds is
  historically reasonable (F11–F13); a ~£3,000 album constant aligns
  with a low-end 1970s album budget, far below prog tier (F14) and above
  the DIY single.
- **Press runs (500–50,000).** Documented runs in this era: 500 (F11),
  1,000 (F13), 2,500 (F12), 5,000 (F15 context) — the low end matches;
  50,000 copies was a hit-level run, not a beginner option.
- **Prices.** Using a single fixed retail price misses the documented
  ladder (F37) and the VAT steps (F40); price changes are era content.
- **Dole and day jobs.** Both are documented (F09, F33) and can carry
  the early-game survival layer without inventing numbers.

## Part 4 — Round 2: what the second pass added

A second research round went at the open questions in
`unresolved-questions.md`. Five of the top six moved. The changes that
matter to design:

### The retail chain is now modelled from real numbers
One 1979 release gives retail, dealer price and margin that reconcile to
the penny (F51): **£5.00 retail → £2.61 dealer ex-VAT → 40% dealer
margin**, against a standard trade term of **36⅔%** (F52). Two
consequences for M5:

- Convert retail to dealer price at **~52% of RRP** (the shop buys at
  ~63% of the ticket, with VAT inside it). Do not invent the split;
  derive it.
- Treat **40% as a competitive weapon** a label can offer a retailer, and
  36⅔% as the baseline. That is a live lever in a promotion campaign.
- The label/distributor split inside the dealer price remains
  undivided (F53) — a tunable, not a fact.

### Representation is two contracts, and the early one costs more
- **Manager: 20% of all earnings, gross**, from an actual 1970 agreement
  recited in a Court of Appeal judgment (F41). No royalty carve-out, no
  expenses-first deduction.
- **Agent and manager are separate instruments**, often signed the same
  day (F42) — model two line items, not one representation slider.
- **Agent: ~10% established, 15–20% for a first agency** (F44). Early
  representation is *dearer*, which gives the player something concrete
  to renegotiate on success.
- The real hazard is **ownership, not percentage**: the manager in the
  O'Sullivan case also owned the publisher (50% after 15% expenses) and
  the label (up to 8%) (F43). Offer that as a choice — bigger cut with no
  ownership, or smaller cut plus publishing.

### Equipment has a real ladder, and it runs the opposite way to expectation
New copy-grade guitars £36.57–£45, a 4-drum kit £122.94, a basic PA
£71.30 (F56); used-professional gear £160–£170 for a guitar or kit
(F57). The finding to design around: **in 1975 a new-but-budget rig
(~£512) was cheaper than a used-professional one (~£688)** (F58), so
bands moved from new cheap to used pro. The upgrade is *quality*, not
novelty — the opposite of the usual shop-ladder.

### Publishing is two splits, not one
The writer keeps ~50% at home but only ~25% of US income, because the
publisher splits 50:50 again between its UK and US companies (F47). The
mechanical rate was 6.25% of retail (F48), and a writer's share on a hit
album side was £900–£1,500 (F49) — hundreds, not thousands. That makes a
£50–£500 advance legible as exploitation rather than abstraction.

### Survival income is a dated step function that gets worse
The single man's dole ran **£9.80 (1975) → £11.10 → £12.90 (Nov 1976)**
against £66.97 average manual pay (F61); it was **contribution-gated**,
so a band of school-leavers qualified for nothing (F62); the statutory
test was being *available for work* — a status risk for a gigging
musician, not an earnings test (F63) — and its real value **fell** from
39.5% of average earnings (1971) to 29.8% (1979) (F64). Model a dated
lookup table, never inflation drift.

### Promotion: cheap at the small end, invisible at the expensive end
Real rate cards: **£6.00–£6.50 a column inch**, 5–8p a word, with series
discounts (F65). Against that, a major spent a reported **£200,000**
launching one album (F66). The small end is priced, the display-page /
plugger / publicist tier is not — which is itself a finding about where
the money went.

### What did not move
Producer points remain unfound after two rounds (F50) — keep any value
out of the game or flag it loudly as a placeholder. Support-slot terms
are still undocumented (F71). The unknown band's night's pay is bounded
but not nailed: £25–£45 per band per night exists, but from **c.1980–84
fieldwork**, and must not be backdated silently (F69).
