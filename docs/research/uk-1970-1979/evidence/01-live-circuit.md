## Dimension 1: Live circuit and career ladder
### Summary
- 1974 UK price tiers (Sounds, 9 Nov 1974): 50p is a "cheap gig", £2 is
  the average for a reasonably big band, £3.50 for a Wembley supergig;
  the promoter takes about a third of the ticket, plus 8% VAT before the
  band's share.
- Established-mid-tier fee economics (Sounds, 1974): 10cc commanded
  ~£1,000 per gig but ~£750 of that was spent before reaching the stage;
  Gallagher & Lyle's self-financed tour cost £15,000 and returned
  ~£3,000 - gross fee is not income. Ticket price was partly
  band-controlled policy (£1 G&L cap; 10cc ~£1.25).
- Festival economics of the era were fragile: Bickershaw 1972 sold £2.25
  tickets with a ~£60,000 gate yet lost money and left acts unpaid; Isle
  of Wight 1970 (600-700k) was declared free and made no profit;
  Glastonbury 1970 charged £1 to just 1,500 people.
- The tiers below headline where unknowns lived: London pub venues (Hope
  & Anchor, Nashville, Tally Ho) from 1973 and band residencies (e.g.
  Stranglers, mid-1976); the college/student-union ents circuit was a
  real booking channel (Bickershaw was staffed and sold via student
  social secretaries).
- Big gap for balancing: no period source located yet for an unknown
  band's per-night fee in pubs/clubs or for support-slot terms (paid vs
  expenses-only); agency commission is only documented in modern sources
  and must not be backdated.

### Tensions & counter-arguments
- Bickershaw's failure has two competing mechanisms: Wikipedia
  emphasises half-price passout resales draining revenue ("the
  organisers lost money"), while the writewyattuk interview stresses the
  main financier's arrest three weeks before and acts booked with no
  money to pay them ("booked an impressive array of acts only to
  discover there was no money to pay them"). Same outcome, different
  cause - model both leakage and counterparty risk.
- Isle of Wight 1970: fan memories say £3 weekend tickets, yet the
  festival was declared free; with free viewing from the hill, paid vs
  total attendance is unknowable. Ticket-price data for 1970 mega-events
  is unreliable.
- Ticket prices were partly band policy, not pure market: Sounds (Nov
  1974) reports 50p-£2 typical and speculates about a coming £5 ticket,
  while G&L capped at £1 and 10cc charged ~£1.25 in the same article.
- All quantified fees located are established acts (10cc, G&L). Nothing
  period-specific yet for the unknown/support tier, and support-slot
  terms (paid vs expenses-only) were not found in period sources; modern
  commission norms (10-25%) must not be backdated into the 1970s.
- Pub rock's "strongest 1972-75" window conflicts with the Stranglers'
  1976 residencies at the same venues - the pub circuit remained the
  entry rung into the punk era; do not model it as closed after 1975.

### Leads & unresolved
- Unknown-tier nightly fees: search Musicians' Union minimum engagement
  scales/rate cards 1970-79, Melody Maker and NME gig-wanted columns,
  and Will Birch's No Sleep Till Canvey Island.
- Working men's club per-night fees: nothing period-specific found
  (Wikipedia WMC page and Pete Brown's clubland blog are non-monetary);
  try club federation yearbooks or the British Newspaper Archive.
- Booking agent commission in the 1970s: only modern guidance found
  (Musicians' Union 2023: agent charges commission on the gig fee under
  a separate agency contract); check Music Week agency ads and
  NEMS/Harry Fox-era contracts.
- Reading Festival and Knebworth 1974/1979 fees and ticket prices
  unverified; the Led Zeppelin 1979 "largest ever fee" claim seen on
  bahrgallery has no figure attached.
- Support slots, pay-to-play and benefit gigs: no period evidence found
  in this pass; a later pass should mine Melody Maker letters pages
  1975-79.
- The academia.edu paper "Student Music" (on student unions as live
  venues in post-war Britain) should be read in full for NUS/SU booking
  rates.
