## Dimension 7: Band pressures and human costs

### Summary
- The dole was a structural income floor at the scene's entry level:
  1976 UK unemployment benefit for a man with a dependent wife and four
  children was £27.50-£34.30/week against average manual earnings of
  £66.97/week, and UB40 (formed Dec 1978) named themselves after the
  signing-on form.
- Day jobs persisted even after a band was gigging and recording: Ian
  Curtis kept a civil-service job at a Manpower Services Commission job
  centre while Joy Division built up (through 1979) - model a held-job
  state that blocks weekday availability and creates a quit-job decision
  point.
- Sustained touring without breaks was an exit trigger, not just a
  fatigue stat: Deep Purple's 1973 treadmill ended in a mid-tour
  resignation and a sacking, while an ordinary working band (Brewer's
  Droop) gigged at roughly 300 shows/year in 1970.
- Family and artistic-control pressure runs alongside fatigue: Gabriel
  left Genesis in 1975 citing family time and a "pseudo-democratic
  committee"; Taylor left the Stones amid credit/royalty talk - two
  distinct morale sinks worth separate mechanics.
- Member-stated reasons can mask money/credit drivers: Taylor publicly
  denied credit/royalty motives while contemporary coverage reported
  exactly that - events should let stated morale reasons diverge from
  hidden ones.

### Tensions & counter-arguments
- Taylor's exit is contradicted across sources: his January 1975 Rolling
  Stone statement insists on "purely musical reasons" and angrily denies
  credit/money talk, yet the same article records industry talk about
  missing co-writing credit and lost royalties, and his wife "hints at
  disenchantment over credits"; later interview paraphrases (iorr.org
  forum, low tier) add fear of drug addiction. Treat the money motive as
  reported-but-contested, not fact.
- The benefit-rate rows cover a family claimant only; the single man's
  rate - the actual punk band-member case - was not located in this
  pass, so dole-vs-gig economics for a teenager cannot yet be
  calibrated.
- Whether gigging while signing on was detected or punished in 1970s UK
  was not documented in anything I found; the "benefit fraud risk" is
  plausible but currently unsourced. A related search on claiming rules
  returned only US/modern material.
- Famous-act bias: four of nine rows are major acts (Gillan, Gabriel,
  Taylor, UB40); only Curtis's day job and Brewer's Droop's gig count
  sit closer to an ordinary unsigned band, and day-job wage levels for
  ordinary musicians remain unknown.

### Leads & unresolved
- 1970s day-job pay rates (factory, clerical, shop work) - check New
  Earnings Survey 1970-79 or Hansard wage tables to price the day job vs
  band trade-off.
- Single claimant unemployment benefit rate 1976-79 and supplementary
  benefit top-ups - Hansard debates and DHSS leaflets; the Supplementary
  Benefits Act 1976 on legislation.gov.uk is a starting point.
- Dole compliance and enforcement for gigging musicians (declaration
  duty, disqualification, signing-on cadence) - Musicians' Union
  archives, period DHSS "Unemployment Benefit" leaflets, punk oral
  histories such as England's Dreaming.
- Split-income mechanics inside 1970s UK bands (equal splits vs writer
  credits, the Taylor royalty dispute) - publishing/royalty evidence and
  the sibling dimensions for gig-fee context.
- Substance use as a documented exit factor (the Taylor accounts here
  are rumour-tier) - Clapton's autobiography, Tony Sanchez's Stones
  book, the 2024 MOJO piece on Taylor, for a sober sourced pass.
- Gig-count benchmarks for more ordinary UK bands - gigographies (e.g.
  quogigography.net) and local press listings to derive gigs/year for
  non-famous acts.
