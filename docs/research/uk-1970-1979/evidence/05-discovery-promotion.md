## Dimension 5: Discovery and promotion

### Summary
- The bottom of the discovery ladder was cheap: a hand-made zine or a
  demo tape could reach gatekeepers directly. Sniffin' Glue went from a
  ~50-copy run to 15,000 monthly within a year and its final issue ran
  20,000 copies on a £90 flexi budget.
- National radio airplay for records was rationed by Musicians' Union
  'needle time', so live BBC sessions (John Peel) became the main on-air
  route for unknown bands - booked directly by the DJ/producer,
  sometimes off a demo tape alone.
- The music weeklies were mass media by comparison: NME 136,000 weekly
  (1971) rising to 230,000 (1980), Melody Maker peaking at 207,000
  (1973), Sounds at 100,000+ by 1972. One review reached six figures of
  fans - the press tier should carry large reach weight.
- TV was chart-gated: Top of the Pops slots tracked songs climbing the
  Top 40, so TV is a late-stage amplifier; regional TV (Granada's So It
  Goes) was the exception that gave unknowns (Sex Pistols) national
  exposure.
- Record shops were both a promotion surface and a financier for
  grassroots product - shops fronted print money and Rough Trade/Virgin
  distribution carried fanzines to shops nationwide.
- Studio time cost money a young band did not have: a new 16-track room
  quoted at £27/hour in Oct 1976, so a multi-hour demo was a serious
  outlay.

### Tensions & counter-arguments
- Sniffin' Glue issue-1 size conflicts: Wikipedia reports an initial run
  of 50 copies, while a Christie's lot description of the first issue
  says "we sold about 300 copies of the first issue"
  (https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-5447429). Print run versus
  actual sales may be the distinction, but no source resolves it.
- The TOTP sales boost is asserted rather than measured: a blog states
  "an appearance meant a dead-cert increase in record sales and
  publicity"
  (https://retro-hen.com/2019/10/12/the-top-of-the-tops-effect), but the
  BBC's own history of pop TV documents only the chart-linked format,
  not uplift - no contemporary quantification exists in the sources
  found this run.
- The circulation compilation is secondary: its author admits smoothing
  gaps in the audit record and using estimates for some periods (Disc
  pre-1963, Record Mirror pre-1967), so 1970s audited figures are usable
  but single-sourced.
- Peel-route evidence is retrospective and success-shaped: a BBC page
  (written after 2004) recalls bands that were booked, while the ignored
  demo senders left no archive - survivorship bias likely.
- So It Goes broadcast date for the Pistols is inconsistent across fan
  and official posts (28 Aug versus 4 Sep 1976) - unverified which is
  correct.

### Leads & unresolved
- Independent Local Radio (Capital/LBC from Oct 1973) playlist power and
  scene plugging were not verified this pass - check IBA archives and
  Capital Radio playlist records; likely a major mid-1970s channel shift
  from BBC-only.
- 1970s music-paper advertising rate cards (cost of a full-page NME/MM
  ad) not found; the ukmix thread notes early-60s rate cards were used
  as a health proxy. Check British Library trade yearbooks.
- Press agent/publicist costs in the 1970s: only modern fee ranges
  surfaced (Musicians' Union and PR-agency pages) - unusable for period
  figures. Check 70s management memoirs or Stiff/Chiswick press budgets.
- Demo-to-label A&R practice beyond Peel (postal submissions versus gig
  sightings, whether tapes were returned) needs primary interviews;
  Stiff's stated £400 (1976) is in run context but the tape pipeline is
  not.
- Flyposting, handbills and gig listings: no verified 1970s data found
  this run - check punk ephemera archives (Roxy/100 Club handbills,
  listings sections).
- Radio 1 needle-time quota figures (a fan history claims 37 max hours
  per week) were not confirmed against an authoritative source - check
  BBC Written Archives or Musicians' Union agreements.
