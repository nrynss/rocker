## Dimension 2: Musician earnings and living costs

### Summary
- Gross gig fees are not income. The only well-documented 1974 figures
  show a hit act (10cc) taking a £1,000 fee per gig with roughly £750
  consumed by road crew, hotels, petrol, vehicles and publicity before
  the band earned anything — model a per-show cost stack, not a per-show
  profit.
- Wage baseline for the whole decade: UK average weekly earnings per
  person ran about £17.51 (1970 Q1) to £74.41 (1979 Q4); full-time men
  in Great Britain averaged £26.8/week (manual) and £35.8/week
  (non-manual) in 1970, both roughly doubling by 1975. This is the
  opportunity cost of the day job a band member quits.
- Absolute living costs were low (1970: pint of lager 20p, 20 cigarettes
  20p, Mini £600; a 1970 Aberdeen ad sells a secondhand Vox AC50 amp for
  £45), so survival on small takings was plausible — but new gear was a
  multiple of secondhand (£184 new Super Twin in 1965).
- Ticket prices bracket the 1970s gig economy: 50p was a cheap gig, £2
  the average for a "reasonably big band" (1974); a £1,000 fee implies a
  roughly 800-1,000 capacity at those prices. Treat the £1,000 figure as
  established-act scale, not a beginner default.
- Inflation (double digits through the mid-1970s) makes every figure
  date-sensitive: a 1972 fee and a 1979 fee of the same nominal size are
  not the same money.

### Tensions & counter-arguments
- "Average weekly wage, 1970" disagrees across sources: £17.51 (Bank of
  England per-person series, includes all employees), £26.8 (manual men,
  New Earnings Survey), £35.8 (non-manual men, NES) and about £32
  (Guardian retrospective). These are definitionally different, not
  contradictory; pick one baseline for the game (recommend NES full-time
  men) and label it.
- The famous-act problem: every verified fee in this pass is for
  successful acts (10cc, Gallagher and Lyle, both 1974). No source was
  found giving a fee for an unknown band; using £1,000 as a beginner's
  fee would badly skew the economy.
- Sources are internally uneasy on whether touring paid: 10cc's manager
  expected the band to be "very much in the red" despite a £1,000 fee,
  and Gallagher and Lyle recovered only £3,000 of a £15,000 outlay — yet
  the latter called it worthwhile because album sales doubled. Gross
  fees and net survival are separate systems; promotion value may
  dominate.
- The MU history site's decade paging appears shifted (the 1971-1980 URL
  returned content headed 1980s), so union minimum rates for the 1970s
  decade could not be confirmed; nearest points are 1961 (£16-£20/week
  West End minimum) and 1985 (£200/week West End minimum) — do not
  interpolate between them.
- A social-media snippet claims a band's fee of £40 for one night at the
  Hope & Anchor with a £40 repair bill, but it was not fetchable and
  likely dates around 1980; it is a line of inquiry, not a finding.

### Leads & unresolved
- Unknown-band and pub-circuit fees 1970-79: unverified. Check Melody
  Maker/Sounds gig listings and promoters' rate cards, plus the Hope &
  Anchor claim in a contemporary source.
- Rehearsal space rents (hourly/weekly, London and regions): nothing
  period-specific found; only 2020s London rates. Try 1970s Yellow
  Pages, Melody Maker classifieds and oral histories.
- UK session musician rates in the 1970s: searches surfaced only modern
  MU/BPI agreements and 1970s US AFM scales (about $10-15/hour, US,
  unverified for UK use). Check the BPI-MU session agreement archive and
  the MU history 1971-1980 chapter via other URL variants.
- Van, PA and drum kit prices 1970-79: not found. Check Beat
  Instrumental and Melody Maker classified archives; the 1970 Vox ad
  shows this material exists in scanned form.
- Actual unemployment benefit amounts (flat-rate) 1970-79: the 1977
  Hansard source gives eligibility thresholds only. Check Hansard and
  DHSS annual abstracts for the weekly flat-rate figures and any
  earnings rules for the partially employed.
- MU minimum engagement rates for dance bands and ballroom work in the
  1970s: likely in MU branch minutes or the "Musicians' Union: A
  History" book; not retrievable from the public site in this pass.
