## Dimension 6: Industry change and genre movements, 1970-1979

### Summary
- The decade splits into two cost regimes: a self-released punk single
  cost £153-£750 (1977) while a top-tier prog album ran £90,000 in
  studio costs (1973) - a budget chasm of ~100-600x that should define
  era-based studio vs DIY mechanics.
- Pub rock (1972-75) cut label break-even to ~2,000 records vs ~20,000
  for mainstream labels, and its scene and personnel (Stiff Records,
  1976; Nick Lowe) became the practical bridge into punk's DIY wave.
- Punk's DIY turn was real but bounded: self-released singles were cheap
  and could profit (Desperate Bicycles made £210 on their first 500),
  but national reach still required distribution - addressed only as
  Rough Trade's network / the Cartel formed (1978); the official indie
  chart arrived 19 January 1980, after the decade.
- The singles market did not collapse by units in the 1970s: BPI trade
  figures show record years in 1978 (88.8m) and 1979 (89.1m); the
  album-side boom (vinyl album trade value peak £163m, 1978) is a
  value/investment shift, not singles-unit decline.
- Majors reacted within months of punk's press breakthrough with
  reported £40,000-£100,000 advances to the hottest acts - a
  signing-spree mechanic with high churn risk (Sex Pistols dropped by
  EMI after ~3 months).

### Tensions & counter-arguments
- Singles-decline framing vs BPI data: Official Charts history shows
  singles unit sales hitting successive records in 1978 (88.8m) and 1979
  (89.1m). Any "singles collapse" mechanic should be value- and
  margin-driven for 1970-79, not unit-driven; the album boom is a
  spend/investment shift alongside growing singles volume
  (https://www.officialcharts.com/who-we-are/seventies-history).
- DIY costs vs DIY rhetoric: Bestley's academic study accepts cheap
  self-released records but argues the empowerment narrative was partial
  - groups remained dependent on national distribution and established
  manufacturing, and cites "the success (and subsequent collapse) of the
  Cartel independent distribution network"
  (https://journals.openedition.org/cadernosaa/pdf/1219).
- Punk advance figures are soft: both the Pistols' ~£40,000 (alleged)
  and the Clash's £100,000 are press-reported, not contract-documented;
  treat them as order-of-magnitude for the signing spree
  (https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/sex-pistols-emi-contract-timeline and
  https://www.hotpress.com/culture/day-1977-clash-signed-cbs-records-100000-22765340).
- Album-cost claims disagree in scope: a JSTOR snippet of Frith says the
  average rock album cost $70,000-$100,000 in studio time by the end of
  the 70s (US-leaning average, basis unstated), far below or above the
  UK £90,000 top-act figure depending on scope; do not merge them into
  one number (https://www.jstor.org/stable/25112581).
- Cartel chronology is fuzzy: Crack dates the Cartel to 1978 while
  Wikipedia describes it as an association that "later emerged" from
  Rough Trade's distribution arm - the sequencing of Rough Trade
  distribution vs the Cartel is unresolved
  (https://crackmagazine.net/article/long-reads/rough-trade-feature).
