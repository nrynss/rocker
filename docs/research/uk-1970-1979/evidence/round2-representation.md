# Round 2: Agent and manager commissions, 1970s UK

Scope: representation percentages only (booking agent, personal
manager). Live fees for unknown bands, producer/publishing terms,
equipment and retail splits belong to sibling researchers and are not
covered here.

### Verdicts

- **Agent: Partially resolved.** The percentage is well supported (10%
  for an established act; 15–20% for a young band starting with an
  agency), but no UK *primary* 1970s document stating the rate was
  found. Music Week's news and display advertising in the sampled
  1977–80 issues state no terms at all. The "who pays" question is
  documented only in a US handbook: the fee is added on top and paid by
  the buyer (promoter), not deducted from the artist's money.
- **Manager: Resolved at the top end, partially for the norm.** A Court
  of Appeal recital of an actual 1970 UK management agreement gives
  **20% of *all* earnings** — gross, spanning record, live and other
  income, with production thrown in free. Whether 20% was typical or a
  star-level rate is not settled: the first pass established ~10% as the
  going norm c.1962–67, so the usable 1970s UK band is 10–20% of gross,
  not a single number.

### What this changes for the game

- **Two separate contracts, two separate cuts.** The O'Sullivan file
  shows a management agreement *and* a sole agency agreement signed the
  same day, renewed together as "Management & Agency Services". Model
  them as distinct deals with distinct percentages (agent ~10%, manager
  10–20%), not one "representation" slider.
- **Manager commission is on gross "all earnings", not net.** The 1970
  clause reads "20% on all earnings", with production absorbed free. Do
  not let the player assume expenses come off first — that is the trap
  the case is famous for.
- **The agent's cut is smaller and arrives earlier.** ~10% of the fee,
  and the 1977 handbook has the promoter paying it on top. In sim terms:
  agent takes its slice at the point of booking; manager takes its slice
  of everything that lands, which means the manager's income scales with
  the agent's success.
- **The real danger is ownership, not the percentage.** The manager who
  took 20% also owned the publisher (50% royalty after 15% expenses) and
  the label (up to 8%). Offer the player the choice: a bigger percentage
  with no ownership, or a smaller percentage plus a slice of publishing.
- **Unknown bands pay more, not less.** The 1991 trade book puts a first
  agency at 15–20% — so early-game representation should be *worse* than
  the 10% an established act commands. This also gives the player
  something concrete to renegotiate on success.

### Still unresolved

- **A contemporary UK statement of the agent's standard rate.** Not
  found in four Music Week issues, archive.org full-text, or Google
  Books. Would be settled by: Music Week 1970–79 concert-agency display
  ads (NEMS, Kennedy Street, Clive Underhill, Harry Fox, MAM) and the
  *Melody Maker* classified/agency pages — the ads exist but state no
  terms in the issues sampled; a targeted sweep of January issues
  (agency directories) is the best next move.
- **Whether the agent charged the promoter as well as the artist ("both
  ends").** Only the 1977 US handbook hints at buyer-pays. Would be
  settled by: a UK agency's standard terms of business, or the Concert
  Promoters' Association / Musicians' Union agency agreements held at
  the Modern Records Centre (Warwick) — MU files on agency registration
  are the specific archive.
- **The 10% vs 20% manager norm for ordinary UK acts.** The 20% figure
  is a star client of a star manager; the 10% norm is 1962–67. Would be
  settled by: a run of standard-form management contracts in the Gilbert
  O'Sullivan case file, or Music Managers Forum / Peter Grant biography
  corroboration (Napier-Bell and Grant full texts returned zero
  full-text hits — both are lending-restricted).
- **Music Week coverage gap.** 1975 issues all 404'd; a full index of
  available dates could not be scraped (directory listing returns 403).
  Would be settled by fetching the site's year index pages with a
  browser session rather than curl.
