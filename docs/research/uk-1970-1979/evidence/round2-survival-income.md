## Round 2: The dole, day jobs and survival income

### Verdicts

- **Single claimant rates: Partially resolved.** Three consecutive
  documented points for a single man now pin the 1975–76 run of the
  series: **£9.80/week** (statutory rate from April 1975),
  **£11.10/week** (in force by June 1976, i.e. from the November 1975
  uprating), **£12.90/week** (from week beginning 15 November 1976). The
  1970–74 start of the decade and 1977–79 remain undocumented in this
  pass. Supplementary benefit top-up scale rates are only partly
  resolved (one documented single-householder figure for November 1976).
- **Signing-on rules: Partially resolved.** The statutory conditions are
  documented precisely (capability/availability, three waiting days,
  earnings-related supplement from day 13 capped at 156 days,
  contribution conditions). Whether gig income specifically was
  declarable, what the disregard was, and whether enforcement/fraud risk
  attached to gigging musicians: **not documented in this pass.** No
  musician penalty case found.
- **Day-job pay: Unresolved.** No occupational wage breakdown (shop
  assistant, clerical, factory labourer) was obtained. The New Earnings
  Survey occupational tables were not reached. Only the pass-1 national
  manual average stands (£66.97/week, Oct 1976).

Confidence in the three documented single-man rates: **high** (primary
statute plus a ministerial uprating statement). Confidence in everything
about gigging-while-claiming: **low — absence of evidence, not evidence
of absence.**

### What this changes for the game

- **The dole is a step function with a sting in the tail.** Ten
  upratings across the decade, none in 1970, and the real value *fell*:
  benefit for a couple went from 39.5% of average earnings in 1971 to
  29.8% in 1979. Survival on the dole should get harder as the decade
  runs, even as the cash number rises. Model the rate as a dated lookup
  table, never as inflation-linked drift.
- **Two separate gates, and the second one bites.** Contribution-based
  eligibility means a member who has never held a waged job qualifies
  for nothing — the 1977 rules demanded Class 1 contributions on 25× the
  £15 lower earnings limit (~£375 of earnings) plus a 50× second
  condition. Day jobs are therefore not just income; they *buy* dole
  entitlement. A band of kids straight out of school gets no dole at
  all.
- **"Available for work" is the risk mechanic, not "earnings".** The
  statutory condition documented in 1977 is being *capable of, and
  available for*, work — unchanged since 1973. That is a status test: a
  member who is effectively a full-time touring musician can be judged
  unavailable, losing entitlement regardless of what they earned.
  Gigging should carry a **status-risk** roll (availability), separate
  from any earnings-declaration arithmetic.
- **Every dole spell starts three days short, and the top-up expires.**
  Three waiting days per spell, and earnings-related supplement only
  from day 13 and only for 156 days. So returning to the dole after a
  tour pays nothing for the first three days, and the generous
  early-dole period (the £27.50→£34.30 effect in pass-1) is a decaying
  resource — a natural timer that pushes a band back toward gigging or a
  day job.
- **A single member's realistic 1976 floor is about £12.90/week against
  £66.97 average manual pay** — roughly a fifth of a labourer's wage,
  before any supplementary benefit top-up. That gap is the whole
  early-game pressure: the dole is survivable only in shared/squatted
  housing or with a top-up, which is exactly the documented scene
  condition.

### Still unresolved

- **Single-claimant rates for 1970–July 1974 and Nov 1977–Nov 1979.**
  Settle via the annual **Social Security Benefits Up-rating Orders**
  (legislation.gov.uk lists them for 1975–1979, two per year: UK SI + NI
  SR) — each Order's Schedule states the new weekly rates verbatim. Not
  reached in this pass: legislation.gov.uk blocks scripted fetches and
  needed one-at-a-time `fetch_content` calls.
- **Supplementary benefit scale rates by year for a single
  householder**, and the rent allowance on top. Settle via the
  **Supplementary Benefits Commission annual reports** and DHSS **Social
  Security Statistics** tables (archive.org holds 1973–1981 volumes, but
  their OCR text is lending-restricted and full-text search is blocked;
  a reading-room or ILL copy, or the SBC reports, would settle it).
- **Whether gig income was declarable and what the disregard was.** The
  single most valuable missing item. Lead: Hansard **"Unemployment
  Benefit (Disregards)", 28 January 1972** — the exact subject, but the
  URL 404s on the historic-Hansard service; find it through the modern
  hansard.parliament.uk search by date. Also DHSS leaflet **UB 1 /
  "Unemployment Benefit"** and the **Unemployment Benefit Regulations**
  on legislation.gov.uk.
- **Enforcement and fraud risk for musicians.** Nothing found. Leads:
  the 1974 debates on "Supplementary Benefit (Fraudulent Claims)" (12
  Nov 1974, historic Hansard); **Musicians' Union** archives on members
  and the dole; British Newspaper Archive for local prosecutions of
  musicians.
- **Day-job pay by occupation.** No figure obtained. Settle via **New
  Earnings Survey** occupational tables 1970–79 (published annually by
  HMSO/Department of Employment; the pass-1 averages came from here but
  not the occupational breakdowns). Also the **Department of Employment
  Gazette** for shop/clerical/manual distributions.
- **Whether employers accommodated gigging** (shift-swapping, part-time,
  cash-in-hand). No evidence either way; anecdotal memoir material would
  be the entry point.
