# Resolution 3: Management, producer and publishing terms

Scope: 1970s UK deal terms, with adjacent-period evidence flagged
explicitly where it is non-UK or outside the 1970s. Every figure below
comes from a page actually fetched during this pass.

## Target 1 — management commission %
**Verdict: Partially resolved.** Strong period UK evidence exists for
the 1960s (customary ~10% of income; 25% of gross at the top end), and
one adjacent 1975 figure (20% of gross, Australia). No direct UK-1970s
management commission percentage was located. The modern 15–25%-of-gross
guidance must NOT be backdated.

**Evidence:**
1. UK, 1962–67, The Beatles / Brian Epstein (the NEMS benchmark):
- Jan 1962 five-year contract: "giving Epstein 10 to 15 per cent of
  their income."
- Oct 1962 renegotiation: "15, 20, or 25 per cent of revenues, depending
  on how much he helped the band earn."
- One contract (post-1962 renegotiation, exact instrument not stated in
  source): "a management commission of 25 per cent of the group's gross
  income after a certain financial threshold had been reached."
- The era's customary rate, per Peter Isherwood's recollection: "Epstein
  took 25 per cent of the gross income, and not the 10 per cent that he
  believed most other managers received at that time." — i.e. ~10% was
  considered the norm by managers themselves.
- By the 1967 renewal: "lower his management fee from 25 to 10 per
  cent."
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Epstein (fetched
  2026-10-16). Tier: secondary (encyclopedia, cites specific sources);
  base in each case is income/revenues/gross income, not royalties
  specifically.
2. 1974/75, AC/DC / Michael Browning (adjacent — Australia, mid-1970s,
   but the closest period manager-commission number found):
- "After a six-month 'trial', I'd be on a straight 20 per cent
  commission of the gross income from then onwards." (memoir extract by
  the manager himself)
- Source: https://au.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/the-wild-side-529
  (fetched 2026-10-16). Tier: primary (manager's memoir), but non-UK.
3. Dead ends: Peter Grant Wikipedia page as retrieved contained no
   commission percentage (regex sweep for "per cent|percent|commission"
   returned zero matches); the Led Zeppelin 1968 contract coverage found
   is the Atlantic record deal, not a management deal, and is out of
   scope.

**Reading for the game:** period UK managers were commonly on ~10% of
income with 20–25%-of-gross as the aggressive upper end; where sources
state a base it is consistently gross income, not record royalties.
Confidence: medium-high for the 10%/25% contrast (single but specific
secondary source); medium for any 1970s carry-over.

## Target 2 — producer points
**Verdict: Unresolved (genuine gap).** No period document, interview or
trade-press figure was located stating a 1970s producer
percentage/points rate for a UK production deal, on retail or on
royalties. Only modern guidance exists in the sources found.

**Evidence:**
1. Qualitative period-adjacent evidence only — Shel Talmy on creating
   the independent-producer model in Britain (early 1960s): "there was
   no such thing, except for me, as an independent producer. All
   producers were on staff, earning a decent wage but certainly not an
   extensive one, with no royalties" and "I said 'I'm an independent
   producer. I get this amount per week and also I get royalties.'" No
   percentage is given anywhere in the interview.
- Source: http://www.classicbands.com/ShelTalmyInterview.html (fetched
  2026-10-16). Tier: primary (producer's own recollection).
2. Modern-only figures found in search snippets (NOT fetched, NOT period
   evidence, must not be backdated): "2 to 4 'points'" as a norm and
   points taken "either on the suggested retail price (SRLP) or
   published price to dealer (PPD)" (lawyerdrummer.com); "3% to 4% of
   the record's sales price" (musiccontracts.com); 3–4 points typical /
   2–5 range (royaltyexchange.com). These establish that the
   retail-vs-royalty base distinction the brief mentions is real in
   modern practice, but no fetched source gives any period number.
3. Dead ends: Sound On Sound Mickie Most profile as retrieved contains
   no royalty percentages (the only money detail is Most self-financing
   acts and "offering them a royalty and a deal" — no rate); searches
   for Visconti/Dudgeon/Chapman/Chinn producer point figures returned
   nothing period-specific.

**Honest gap statement:** a documented 1970s UK producer "point" value
was not found in this pass. Any game figure would have to be flagged as
unverified or sourced from later practice.

## Target 3 — publishing advances
**Verdict: Partially resolved.** One concrete 1970s UK advance figure
(£50, 1974, novice standard-form deal) plus solid confirmation of the
era's structural norms (full-term copyright assignment; 50:50 revenue
split). No "typical advance range" for a new songwriter was found — only
the exploitative minimum litigated in the courts.

**Evidence:**
1. Schroeder Music Publishing Co Ltd v Macaulay [1974] 1 WLR 1308 (House
   of Lords) — the standard-form novice deal:
- "the plaintiff's remuneration under the agreement would be limited to
  a £50 advance payable thereunder during the five-year period."
- "This was to last five years and could be automatically extended for
  five years if the royalties went above £5000."
- "The global copyright was assigned to another party in return for a
  fixed percentage of any royalties."
- Source:
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schroeder_Music_Publishing_Co_Ltd_v_Macaulay
  (fetched 2026-10-16). Tier: secondary summary of a primary court
  judgment; the £50 is the case's own finding on the contract.
2. 1970s norms, publisher-side memoir (Simon Napier-Bell, excerpting
   'Black Vinyl White Powder'):
- "In the 70s, when Elton signed the original contract, it was normal
  for publishers to take writer's songs for the life of copyright."
- "Firstly, in the UK, where Elton and Dick James split the royalties
  50/50; then again in America, where Dick James's UK company and Dick
  James's American company also split the royalties 50/50. That meant,
  in the USA Dick James got a total of 75% of each song and Elton only
  25%. In the 1970s, this sort of set-up was quite normal."
- Source:
  https://simonnapierbell.substack.com/p/dick-james-and-the-joy-of-publishing
  (fetched 2026-10-16). Tier: secondary (insider memoir). Caveat:
  Napier-Bell dates Elton's signing to "the 70s" while the DJM contract
  is usually dated 1967/68 — treat the described norms as 1960s–70s; the
  50/50 UK split matches the gov.uk streaming report norm already
  verified (mission context).

**Reading for the game:** the structure is well evidenced — life-of-copyright
assignment on a 50:50 split — but **no advance range was found**. The only
figure available is the £50 in *Schroeder v Macaulay*, and that is a
**litigated floor**: a contract so exploitative the House of Lords voided it,
which is the worst case rather than a typical one. Earlier text here proposed
"£50–£100" as a range and called £50 typical; that is not supported and is
withdrawn. Use £50 as the game's illustrative worst-case deal, and treat the
mid-range advance as still unresearched (see `unresolved-questions.md` #4).
Confidence: high for the structural norms, **low** for any specific advance.
